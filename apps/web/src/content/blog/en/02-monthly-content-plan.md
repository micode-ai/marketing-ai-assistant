Start from what you already have, not from an empty calendar. Audit last month, pick three or four themes that connect to what you actually sell, set a cadence you could sustain in your worst week, then map each channel to the formats it rewards and fill the remaining slots. A month of content takes an afternoon to plan and roughly one working day a month to produce, provided the planning happens first.

The reason most plans die is not ambition, it is that they were built as a list of post ideas rather than as a system that produces post ideas. What follows is the second kind.

## Step 1: Audit the month you just finished

Before you plan anything, write down what you published and what it did. Not to build a report — to find out what you actually make. Most teams discover that they publish far more product news than they thought and far less of the practical material their audience engages with.

Pull the last month into five columns: date, channel, theme, format, and the one number that channel cares about. Then count. Three questions answer themselves:

- **Which theme did you publish most?** Usually it is whatever was happening internally, not what the audience asked for.
- **Which post outperformed the rest, and why?** If you cannot say why, that is the finding.
- **What did you plan and not publish?** The gap between intention and output is your real cadence.

## Step 2: Pick three or four themes, no more

A theme is a recurring subject you can write about ten times without repeating yourself, and that has a plausible line to a sale. Three or four is the working number: fewer and the feed gets monotonous, more and you never build recognition for any of them.

Good themes tend to fall into four buckets, and a balanced month draws from all of them:

| Bucket | What it does | Roughly how much of the month |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Practical how-to | Earns attention from people who have the problem | 40% |
| Point of view | Makes you distinguishable from competitors | 20% |
| Proof and product | Explains what you do and shows it working | 25% |
| Behind the scenes | Makes a small company feel like people | 15% |

Percentages are a starting shape, not a rule. The point is that a month made entirely of product announcements has no reason for anyone to follow it, and a month with no product in it never converts.

## Step 3: Set a cadence you can survive

Pick the number of posts you could publish during the worst week of the month — the week with the launch, the outage, or the holiday. That is your cadence. Anything above it is a bonus, not a commitment.

For a team of one or two, two or three posts a week on one or two primary channels is realistic and consistent. Consistency matters more than volume because it is what lets you read your own analytics: if the cadence is erratic, every change in the numbers is explained by the cadence rather than by the content.

## Step 4: Map channels to formats

Each channel rewards a different shape, and the same idea should take a different form in each. This is the table worth keeping on the wall:

| Channel | What it rewards | Format for one idea | Realistic cadence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LinkedIn | Specifics, first-person experience | 120–200 words, no link in the body | 2–3 per week |
| Facebook | Conversation and local relevance | Short post plus one image | 2–3 per week |
| Instagram | Visual proof, saves | Carousel of steps, or a single strong image | 3–4 per week |
| Threads | Fast, conversational takes | One or two sentences, a question at the end | Daily if you can |
| TikTok | Video only — there is no text-only post | 20–40 second demonstration, or a photo post | 1–3 per week |
| Telegram | Depth for people already following | Longer note, links welcome | 1–2 per week |

Note the TikTok row. It is the constraint that most often breaks a plan built in a spreadsheet: if you have not planned to shoot anything, you cannot post there at all.

## Step 5: Build the calendar and fill the gaps

Now assemble it, in this order:

1. **Put the fixed dates in first.** Launches, events, holidays, anything already committed.
2. **Assign a theme to each week.** One theme per week keeps the writing focused; rotate the buckets from step 2.
3. **Place the anchor pieces.** One substantial piece per week — the article, the video, the carousel — that the rest of the week derives from.
4. **Derive the small posts from the anchors.** A single how-to yields a LinkedIn post, three Threads posts and an Instagram carousel without becoming repetitive.
5. **Count the empty slots and fill them from the audit.** Posts that worked before are legitimate material; reuse the idea in a new format rather than reposting the text.
6. **Write the reason next to each slot.** One line: why this, why here. In four weeks it is the only thing that will let you judge the plan.

Two hours in, you should have a month of slots where every slot has a theme, a format, a channel and a reason. The writing is a separate session, and it goes fast because none of the decisions are left.

## What to do when the month goes wrong

It will. The rule is to cut scope, not quality. Drop the least important theme entirely rather than publishing thin versions of everything. Keep the cadence on your primary channel and let the secondary ones lapse for a week — an irregular feed on one channel costs less than a mediocre feed everywhere.

Keep a small reserve, too: two or three finished, undated posts that are not tied to anything topical. They exist for the week that falls apart.

## Close the month before you plan the next one

Spend thirty minutes at the end of the month with the plan and the numbers side by side. Which slots were filled, which were abandoned, which theme actually earned attention, and which channel took effort out of proportion to what it returned.

That review is the audit from step 1 for next month, which is what makes this a loop rather than a fresh start every four weeks. The second month is always easier to plan than the first, and by the third the themes largely choose themselves.

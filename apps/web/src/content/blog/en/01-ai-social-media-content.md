Brief the assistant the way you would brief a new copywriter on their first day: who the reader is, what the product actually does, what you want the reader to do next, and two posts of yours that already worked. Then keep the opening line and the call to action for yourself. An AI draft is dependable in the middle — the explanation, the structure, the second and third paragraphs — and generic at the edges, which is exactly where voice lives.

Everything below is the workflow that makes that repeatable rather than lucky. It assumes you are one person or a small team writing for several channels in more than one language, which is the case where a blank editor costs the most time.

## What the assistant needs before it writes a word

Most robotic output is a briefing problem, not a model problem. A model given three sentences of context has to fill the gap with the average of everything it has read, and the average of the internet is enthusiastic, abstract and slightly American. Give it specifics and the average never gets a chance to surface.

| What to give it | A concrete example | What you get without it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The audience, narrowly | Founders of 5–20 person software companies who do their own marketing | A post addressed to "businesses" that lands with nobody |
| What you sell, in plain words | A tool that writes, schedules and measures social posts in three languages | Vague benefit language: "streamline your workflow" |
| The one action | Open the guide, reply with a question, book a call | Three competing calls to action in one post |
| Voice rules, as bans | Second person, no exclamation marks, never "unlock" or "game-changer" | The default register: excited, adjective-heavy |
| Proof you actually have | The six networks you publish to, the languages you write in | Invented statistics and fabricated case studies |
| Channel and length | LinkedIn, 120–180 words, no hashtags | A post written for no platform in particular |
| Two posts that worked | Paste the raw text of both | An imitation of every brand rather than yours |

The last row does more than the rest combined. Examples transmit rhythm, sentence length and the jokes you are willing to make in a way that no adjective list can.

## The workflow, step by step

Work in one direction — brief, draft, cut, personalise, schedule — and never let the model do the last step. Each of these takes a minute or two once the project context exists.

1. **Write the brief once per project, not per post.** Audience, product, voice bans and proof points change quarterly at most. Store them where the assistant reads them automatically.
2. **State the job of this specific post.** "Explain why our analytics show period growth instead of lifetime totals" is a job. "Write a post about analytics" is not.
3. **Ask for three drafts, not one.** Three drafts from one brief show you the range of the idea. Picking is faster than fixing.
4. **Cut the first two sentences.** In most drafts the model warms up before it starts. The post usually begins at sentence three.
5. **Replace the opening line with something only you could write.** A number from your own dashboard, a question a customer actually asked, a mistake you made last month.
6. **Rewrite the call to action in your own words.** Models default to polite, low-commitment endings. Say the specific thing you want.
7. **Check every factual claim against something real.** If the draft asserts a percentage, a customer count or a comparison to a competitor, either source it or delete it.
8. **Schedule it, and note why you scheduled it then.** The reason is what you will read when you review the month.

## What stays human

Three things do not survive delegation: the opening line, the call to action, and any claim about reality. The first two carry your voice and your intent. The third carries your credibility, and a language model has no way of knowing whether your product does the thing it just said it does.

There is a fourth, quieter one: the decision about what to post at all. A model asked for a content idea will produce a plausible one. Whether that idea is worth your audience's attention this week is a judgement about your market, and it is the part of the job that is actually yours.

## Keeping a brand voice across dozens of posts

Voice survives when it is written down as constraints rather than adjectives. "Friendly but professional" means nothing to a model, and honestly not much to a person. What works is a short list of mechanical rules: sentence length, whether you use second person, whether you use em dashes, which words are banned, whether you ever open with a question, how you refer to your own product.

Keep that list in the project context so it applies to every generation, and revise it when you catch yourself editing the same thing three times. If you rewrite "leverage" out of every draft, add it to the ban list instead of rewriting it a fourth time.

## The same post in three languages is three posts

A translated post reads like a translated post, and in Polish or Russian that is immediately obvious to a native reader. Idioms do not carry, the search phrases people actually type are different, and the register that reads as confident in English can read as pushy elsewhere.

The fix is to brief once and generate per language, letting each version choose its own examples and phrasing while keeping the same claim and the same call to action. Then have someone read each one out loud. If a sentence is hard to say, it was translated rather than written.

## Before you publish

A short pass that catches most of what goes wrong:

- Does the first line work with no context, in a feed, next to something funnier?
- Is there exactly one thing you want the reader to do?
- Is every number and claim traceable to something real?
- Would a colleague guess you wrote it?
- Does it fit the channel — length, formatting, whether links are penalised there?

If a draft fails the last question, it is usually faster to regenerate it for that channel than to reshape it by hand. That is the honest division of labour: the model handles the variations, you handle the judgement.
